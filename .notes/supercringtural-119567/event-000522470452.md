# Sequential Tart's interview to Sera Gamble

Interview by Mary Borsellino published the 01/12/2006 concerning the show's theme, the writting process, her writing of erotica, poetry and her writing overall. 

The interview goes as follows:

Sequential Tart: Eric Kripke's offered a variety of answers in interviews as to what Supernatural is for him, the most often-quoted probably being "Star Wars in truck stop America". Is that the interpretation you approach it with, or do you have a different take?

Sera Gamble: I sometimes say it's "the epic love story of Sam and Dean," but that's just to tease Eric. Yes, I do keep the "Star Wars on Route 66" thing in mind. However huge the scope of Star Wars got, it never stopped being a personal story that was very simple and family-centered at its core.

It's the "truck stop America" part that I especially like. Our show has a different feel than anything else on TV. That dusty road, the vintage car, the classic rock. Oh, and the mullets. I'm a huge fan of mullets and the characters who wear them. I love the juxtaposition of that stuff with the supernatural.

ST: Could you explain a little of the process that each Supernatural episode goes through as it's written? How much is dictated by the season's broader story? Are contributions made by the show's other writers?

SG: "Show mythology" episodes have to be crafted carefully to fit the broader arc. With closed-ended episodes it's not as important, but we do keep in mind where the boys are emotionally and service that. This season, they lost their father. That colors their points of view, even when they're just hunting the monster of the week.

Here's the basic process: the writers sit down with the executive producers and pitch a bunch of ideas. My style is to just keep talking until Eric's look of "you're on crack" is replaced by the faraway, happy look of "this could potentially make people pee themselves with fear".

Once we've settled on stories, we each disappear into our offices and start plotting them out. Occasionally a story unfolds quickly and elegantly � "Bloodlust" was like that. Others are evil, sadistic, slow-moving beasts. We tend to drift into each other's offices when we get stuck. We're very passive-aggressive, though. We never say we need help � instead, we offer to buy each other delicious Starbucks coffee. Then once everybody's caffeinated, we pounce.

One of the exec producers oversees the development of each episode, and ultimately, Eric spends up to a week refining it and making sure it fits into the bigger story. After all that, we finally write the actual script. Does that make it all sound incredibly tedious and boring? It's actually not bad, and sometimes it's so funny and weird I can't believe it's my job. Like when I spend an hour discussing the ways to get someone to die by dunking their own head in a deep fryer. (There are more ways than you think.)

ST: Do you do any writing exercises with the Supernatural universe, to help you learn the characters and the world outside of the confines of particular episode stories? If not, do you have other techniques for getting the characters right?

SG: I reread Eric's scripts before I start writing. It's actually been quite challenging to get the boys right. I didn't realize how few average-Joe, heterosexual 20-something guys I've even hung out with until I started writing Sam and Dean. I mean, I come from the theater! I don't even know any men who change their own oil! So now I pay very close attention to boys like that. I am a world-class eavesdropper.

ST: Your episode 'Faith' raised the bar regarding what subject matter the show was prepared to address. Where did the idea for that story originate? Aspects of it reminded me of Daniel Knauf's show Carnivale � was this an influence?

SG: Raelle Tucker and I were writing partners last season. We used to make separate lists of possible episode areas and then cherry-pick the best. We'd both written down "faith healing", so it jumped out at us. Then we discussed making it scary. To me, Death is the scariest monster out there. Isn't that what we're really running from, no matter what's chasing us? So we tossed that in � the idea that the faith healings are really caused by a Grim Reaper someone is controlling, and the idea that you can't kill a Reaper no matter what. We pitched it to Bob Singer, one of the exec producers. He loved it, because he saw the potential for genuine conversation about faith and organized religion. He's a pretty deep, philosophical guy; he always wants to find subtle ways to talk about real issues.

So Bob called Eric into the room. Both of them are directors as well as writers, so the best way to hook 'em is to make them see some kind of scary, cool scene in their head � something they weren't expecting. Raelle and I hadn't gotten that far in the brainstorming yet. But Bob proceeded to pitch the greatest teaser, just off the top of his head. He pulled that whole "we open at the climax of another case, and Dean goes down" thing out of nowhere. Raelle and I just stared with our mouths open, because we knew at that moment the episode was going to kick ass.

(I don't recall Carnivale coming up while we were figuring out "Faith." I was dating a preacher's son at the time, though, so I had more immediate inspiration.)

ST: Your writing career has carried strong strains of sex positivity and feminism in it, neither of which have traditionally had a fit in the kinds of horror tropes that Supernatural's premise is built on. Have there ever been ideology clashes between story and storyteller?

SG: No, I haven't at all been bothered by the classical "horror tropes." It all seems kind of funny to me. I see it as light entertainment. I suppose if I didn't, I wouldn't be working on the show. But I really dig blood and guts and gore. I think it's cool when the girl gets chomped, and I think it's cool when the guy gets chomped. When I write an episode, I try to distribute the beatings and eviscerations as fairly as possible.

I'm much more likely to be offended by a show that's hypocritical � that purports to be feminist or spiritual or risqu� but, underneath the bells and whistles, isn't. Supernatural is exactly what it says it is, from the muscle car to the lady in the nightgown skewered on the ceiling.

ST: I'd like to hear more about your upcoming (though, by the time this interview's online, it'll have aired, so don't worry about being shy with spoilers) episode 'Crossroad Blues', since Robert Johnson and devil dog mythology are both completely awesome things, and even if I wasn't already convinced of your fabulousness I would be just for that. Where'd your creation of the story begin?

SG: I can't really take credit for the fabulousness, because Eric has wanted to tell the Robert Johnson story for years. It's been up on a board in the writers' room since day one, up for grabs � whoever can find a way to work the legend into an episode gets to write it. I latched onto it right away. I was determined � I think I pitched three or four different versions for Season One. (They sucked.) But then John Winchester made his deal with the Demon in the season two premiere, and suddenly there was a lot to talk about on the subject of deal making. And with John dead � bringing him back was something I believed Dean would consider selling his soul for.

I did a lot of research into Hoodoo to write the episode. Robert Johnson's music is replete with Hoodoo references. It felt right for our show to get further into that kind of eclectic folk magic, since it is distinctly American. And we wanted to introduce some new elements into Sam and Dean's arsenal, to use alongside stuff like holy water and Latin spells.

My favorite part was writing the teaser, where Robert Johnson is killed. It feels strange and wonderful to write about a real person. There's something deeply cool about basing a character description on a photograph, and listening to the guy singing about being chased by hellhounds while you write about him being chased by hellhounds.


ST: Of the episodes you've written, do you have a particular favourite? Were any of them especially difficult to create?

SG: I really dig Gordon, the hunter character in "Bloodlust." We were going to kill him off, but he turned out to be so interesting we kept him alive. It is very satisfying to come up with a good villain. Although honestly, a lot of the credit for Gordon goes to Sterling K. Brown, the actor who plays him. And then, of course, Bob Singer directed him. That's the thing with TV; you can never really take credit for anything. It's all collaborative.

I'm intrigued by the larger questions our show raises � if there's a hell, does that mean there's a heaven? I wrote an episode in which people kill after being visited by something that may or may not be an angel. That, of course, raises lots of questions no one can answer. And it was important to hold onto that ambiguity � to keep the world of the show from spiraling out into Judeo-Christian-land, and also because in life we don't ever get definitive answers to questions of faith. Keeping all that going for a whole episode was trickier than I anticipated. When we were working on it, we would put on that Voice Over Guy voice and go, "This week on Supernatural: hot zombie chicks. And next week, Sam and Dean ponder the age-old question, 'Is there a God?'" It was heavy.

Happily, I'm working on some ideas now that can best be summed up as, "Sam Winchester gets laid."

ST: Between your two season 2 episodes and one by your frequent collaborator Raelle Tucker ('Children Shouldn't Play with Dead Things'), Dean Winchester's gone to some very dark places this season. Are you a writer who enjoys pulling everything out from under a character and getting them down as far as they can fall, or do you get 'J.K. Rowling syndrome' where you feel guilty for the havoc you're wreaking? Have there ever been exceptions to your general view on the matter?

SG: I don't feel guilty at all. We're a horror show. It's our responsibility to kill a lot of people, and torture those who get to live.

ST: Is there any story you really hope to get the chance to tell through Supernatural?

SG: I like really trippy, layered, surreal episodes. I'd like to do an episode with whole scenes that take place inside nightmares. Nightmare on Elm Street completely freaked me out when I was a kid. And it'd be a good excuse to do all that Being John Malkovich unconscious mind stuff. I haven't even mentioned this to Eric yet, so I have no idea if he thinks it'd work on our show or not. He might come into my office after he reads this and tell me to lay off the experimental French cinema and get back to ripping still-beating hearts out of people's chests.

ST: How and when did you realise that writing was what you wanted to do with your life?

SG: Writing about what I experience is completely natural and utterly necessary for me. I've always done it. It's how I process the world, and it's probably why I didn't turn into an insane drug addict or something. But I never wanted to do it for a living, because I didn't want to ruin it � I was afraid of that stereotype of professional writing, that you end up miserable and barely able to look at your computer.

I studied music, then quit that and went to theater school instead. I did a lot of acting in my early 20s, and it kept leading me back to writing. By then I had failed at enough things that I finally valued my ability to write. I realized I was being a little romantic and precious about the whole thing, and that ultimately the point is to just to write as much as possible, as well as possible, and see what you can come up with.

Still, when I got into the business I promised myself that if I started turning into Curmudgeonly Writer Lady and hating it, I'd quit. So far, I still sincerely enjoy writing. I think that might be the real gift � not that I can write, but that I don't suffer too terribly while I'm doing it. I look forward to writing each script. It really annoys some of my friends.

ST: Your story 'Blue Star' explores a particular kind of ennui and dislocation from life that occurs for some people in their early twenties, which is a period more usually depicted in fiction as being a time when young adults get over their teenage angst and start being productive members of society. Was Ari's journey towards accepting the intensity and terror his comparative youth can offer him, based more on personal experience or on a general perception of that age group?

SG: First of all, I think the idea that you get over your teenage angst and go right into being a productive member of society is a crock of shit. There's a whole different thing that happens to people in their early twenties. For me, that included a lot of drifting, indecision, and isolation. Other people I know made a much smoother transition, but pretty much everyone I've talked to says that it was a time marked by tremendous anxiety. The true weight of "what are you doing with your life" falls on you for the first time. And there's a sense of needing to rush, get to where you're going, succeed, prove yourself. For many of us, too, a feeling that we're adults now, and so if we fail it's on us. No more excuses. No more bail-outs.

I probably put some of that stuff into Ari. But I also see him as unusually isolated. He's been on his own since he was very young, and in almost every way he's been living the life of a much older person. He's hardened up by grief. I was interested in the ways that manifested in him, the clues that living that way was making him suffer under the surface. When he actually connects with someone for a little while, it breaks through to what's really in there. He says, oh, this is what it's like to really be 21. Which is a way of saying, this is what it's like to actually invest in something and be disappointed. Which isn't really a sign of age; it's a sign of being a part of the world. I like to think that he realizes that it's a "good hurt." It wakes him up.

ST: What drew you to erotica as a genre?

SG: I've never sat down at the computer with the intention of writing erotica. I don't really know much when I start writing a story. I just start following the characters around, seeing what they'll do. Of course, they all come from my head, so I should probably own up and say I think about sex a lot. It might not be completely coincidental that my characters often jump into bed with each other.

I'm not shy about writing explicitly, but my focus is on what's happening to the characters. I never see those scenes as an opportunity to titillate. I'm so much more interested in sex as a means of illuminating something about a person. I guess that why the sex itself in my stories is so seldom "sexy"; it often turns out pretty awkward or sad or bizarre. I feel a little guilty when people read my stories in volumes of erotica. They're so tame. Don't get me wrong, I don't at all mind being called an "erotica writer." I'm thrilled to be published in places like nerve.com and The Best American Erotica series. It puts me in fantastic company.

ST: Do you still write poetry?

SG: Ha! Yes. Everyone at Supernatural is going to read this and I'll never live it down.

I spent most of my adolescence writing poetry in a caf�. I found myself doing the exact same thing last weekend. All that's really changed is that now I have a car, which I probably would have killed for back then. And I wear maybe 25% less black.

ST: Is it much of a shift from script-writing to screen-writing, both in terms of story plotting and on the basic structure level?

SG: On a basic structure level, you can break TV scripts and film scripts down into nearly identical components. They have a different number of acts, but if you keep slicing them up you end up with a similar template. A film script is twice the length of a TV script, so in theory you could plot it as an epic, super-sized TV episode. In practice, writing a movie feels very different. The scope, the depth, the pace. I just started writing one after three seasons of pounding out TV scripts one after another, and it's a humbling experience. Writing rough drafts sometimes feels like trying to get clean in dirty bath water. My latest strategy is to give myself permission to write the absolute crappiest film script in the history of the world. So far, that's kept it all in perspective.

ST: You mention on your site that you love 'trashy blogs'. What're some of your favourites?

SG: It's probably less love and more addiction, actually. I have a list I click through practically every day. My favorite is gofugyourself.com, a site devoted to trashing celebrity fashion. I went to college with one of the writers. It's so well-written that it barely qualifies as trashy. I always read defamer.com, of course, and then I do a quick and dirty rotation of tabloid blogs. It's probably really unhealthy. Think about how many lies I consume in a week. It's gross. But I doubt I'll stop doing it.
